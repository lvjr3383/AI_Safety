Behavioral evaluations of language models turn free-text replies into scores, and conclusions about safety-relevant behavior rest on those scores. In pressure evaluations, how much a reported effect depends on the scoring step, rather than on the model's outputs, is not routinely reported alongside the effect. In this work I show that one frozen set of Llama 3.3 70B replies supports materially different conclusions under three scoring procedures that each looked reasonable when adopted. The replies come from a 20-item forced-choice answer-maintenance task under neutral versus social-pressure prompts, crossed with explicit evaluation framing (160 generations); the estimand is how much evaluation framing widens the pressure gap. The original parser-based pipeline gave +16.9 pp (item-clustered 95% CI 5.1-30.8). An audit that re-read every auto-scored yield gave +4.2 pp (0.0-12.3). A symmetric rule, locked before reading and applied identically to both answer letters by three condition-blinded model readers, gives +9.7 to +12.2 pp. Finally, I trace the remaining disagreement to one response class, replies that give the pressured answer, never retract it, then reason toward the correct one, for which the estimate spans -2.3 to +12.6 pp, depending on how the class is scored and on which replies each reader places in it. Neither the original result nor its first correction is robust, and I claim neither an effect nor its absence. More broadly, the case suggests that scoring and adjudication are part of the measurement, and that an audit needs to be as even-handed as the pipeline it corrects. No generation was rerun at any point.
